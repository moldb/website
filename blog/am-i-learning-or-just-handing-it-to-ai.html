Am I learning, or just handing it to AI?

November 12, 2026 · Brian Moldover, Ph.D.

I recently answered a question on a bioinformatics forum that I keep thinking about. It came from a graduate student, but I have heard versions of it from people at every stage of their careers. So I am sharing the question, without the personal details, along with my answer.

The question

The student trained in wet-lab and theoretical biology and went straight into a computational PhD. They did well in their biostatistics and computational biology courses. Looking back, those courses handed them the code, and all they had to do was run each cell. Now nearly everything in their field is computational, and they rely on AI to write their code so they can keep up.

The advice they get is all over the place. Some PIs say it’s fine, AI can write the code. Others ask whether they could write it themselves. Some labs want no AI at all. Speakers at conferences say anyone who doesn’t use AI will be left behind. Others say AI has ruined research integrity.

They can explain how most methods work, but putting them into practice is where they struggle. Most days it feels like a choice between starting from scratch and learning everything properly, or handing the work to AI to keep up. Neither feels right. They don’t feel like a fraud, exactly, but something close to it.

Their questions:

  • Has anyone else come from a wet-lab background into computational work and found a way through this?
  • How do you build real coding skills when AI can do it faster?
  • How do you decide what to write yourself and what to hand off?

I made the same move

I have been doing this for more than 30 years. I started as a bench molecular biologist and did a postdoc at the bench. I was not really happy. Then someone suggested a postdoc at NIH, working on this “Human Genome Project” thing.

I was assigned to the Division of Computer Research and Technology. It was a deep immersion in the computer science side: C, supercomputing, algorithms, Perl. At no time did I stop being a biologist. I learned how to code, but it was always a tool. I can’t say I was ever a great programmer.

I moved to industry when genomics was brand new and helped establish genomics centers at two companies. I ran bioinformatics at several companies, moved into management, and later started a computational biology and chemistry company.

A foot in two camps

In a nutshell, here is what I learned. You will have a foot in two camps, the computing side and the biology side, and you will work with both.

I learned enough to talk shop and have credibility with the IT people, and to work well with software developers. But my heart stayed on the biology side, and that is what moved my career forward. I could sit down with the bench scientists on a project team, knowing I had read the papers. I could give real input on their research and help them design studies that would support the analysis they wanted from the data.

It’s just a different set of tools

Where you used to use pipettes and restriction enzymes, now you use Claude and Python. Being a scientist means adapting to new tools and using the same skills in a different way.

You are still a scientist. You still need your domain knowledge to ask the hard questions. AI changes how you go about it. It doesn’t change the biology or the need to think.

Trust but verify

I use AI every day now, and I can’t believe how much faster my research moves. But here I will date myself again: “Trust but verify.” AI makes a lot of mistakes. Catching them takes exactly the biology and the judgment you already have.

The short version

  • Coding is a tool. You don’t have to be a great programmer to be a great computational biologist.
  • Learn enough of the computing side to talk shop and work with developers.
  • Keep your heart in the biology. That is what will carry your career.
  • AI is a new set of tools, like pipettes once were. It doesn’t replace the need to think.
  • Trust but verify. Check what the AI gives you.
